# Honest, glanceable walls win ticket-holder claims

The offer walls that work show every offer at once in a uniform layout, lead each card with a concrete benefit beside the partner's name, let the holder claim in one tap without a new sign-up, and state expiry, cap and conditions in plain text before the tap. The patterns to avoid are the ones that break a promise or hide something: countdowns or "N left" figures that are not literally true, terms buried in tooltips, codes locked in images or sent only by email, carousels that hide later partners, and expired offers that still look claimable. The complaints that dominate public reviews of perk products are about offers failing at the counter, not about layout, so venue-side reliability matters at least as much as card design. Vibe City's closest precedents are festival wristband discount lists and web-based destination passes, not the large ticketing apps, which sell paid add-ons instead of free partner perks. None of the surveyed products was documented showing a visible remaining-claims counter, so a truthful one is a possible differentiator, with the caveat that its effect on claim rate is unmeasured. **This is desk research from public sources, completed on 14 November 2026, with no Vibe City user interviews, analytics or usability tests.** No study found tests an offer wall for event ticket holders directly; the evidence is transferred from e-commerce, grocery couponing, mall mobile-coupon trials and regulator casework, and much of the competitor detail comes from search-result snippets and vendor pages, flagged as such throughout. Treat the recommendations as well-grounded hypotheses to test with real Cebu attendees and partners, not as proven results.

## How to read the evidence in this report

Three kinds of source sit behind the findings, and they deserve different levels of trust. The strongest are pages the researchers opened and read: Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) usability articles, the Princeton dark-patterns crawl, the UK Competition and Markets Authority's Wowcher release, the Chase Offers FAQ, the Entertainer redemption help page, the Manchester Punk Festival discounts page and the W3C timing criterion. Even these are mostly qualitative guidance without sample sizes, not controlled experiments. The middle tier is academic work reached through abstracts, repository records or press coverage and not checked against the original papers; this covers the zero-price, freebie, scarcity meta-analysis and mobile-coupon studies. The weakest tier is search-result snippets of pages that were not opened (most competitor help centres, review sites and brand-guideline documents) and vendor blogs or case studies with a commercial interest. Where a claim below rests on the weakest tier, the text says "snippet" or "vendor".

Several things were not found at all. No public teardown describes the pixel-level anatomy of any surveyed offer card, so logo-tile treatment and badge placement are undocumented. No head-to-head test of benefit-first versus brand-first headlines, code-reveal versus one-tap save, or modal versus inline detail was found. No Philippine regulator guidance on urgency claims was searched for or found; the regulatory material is from the United States, United Kingdom and European Union. The FTC report itself, the CMA open letter and the EU guidance were read through press releases and law-firm summaries, so anyone needing legal sign-off should check the originals.

## Cards that get claimed state the benefit, the brand and the limits

Face value is the best-supported driver of redemption. A two-year mall trial of text coupons, read only at abstract level, found that traditional features such as face value remained the most important factor and that snack-food coupons performed especially well ([Monash record of Danaher et al. 2015](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/where-when-and-how-long-factors-that-influence-the-redemption-of-)). NN/g's qualitative testing adds that "users were suspicious if discounts were not clearly listed" and that it is clearer to list an item simply as free than to show a charge and subtract it ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/applying-discounts/)). Together these support leading each card with the concrete benefit ("Free latte", "20% off your bill") instead of the partner's name. That ordering is an inference; no study found tests it directly, and the festival precedents do the opposite, heading each entry with the venue name.

**"Free" is the best-evidenced frame.** Research on zero prices found that the share choosing an option rises far more than cost-benefit logic predicts when its price drops to zero, an effect the authors attribute mainly to affect and which faded when subjects were made to deliberate ([Boston Fed working paper](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/research-department-working-paper/2006/how-small-is-zero-price-the-true-value-of-free-products.aspx)). The exact percentages are reported inconsistently across secondary sources, so none is quoted here. A second study, reached through trade-press coverage, found that people later valued an item more when it had been offered free with a purchase than when it had been offered at a deep discount, because a free item's value is inferred from the brand giving it ([CosmeticsDesign-Europe](https://cosmeticsdesign-europe.com/Article/2013/08/21/Study-finds-freebies-increase-brand-value-perception-for-consumers)). The practical reading for Vibe City: where a partner can afford it, a free low-cost item will probably out-pull a small discount, and the partner's name and logo should sit right beside the benefit because the brand is what gives the freebie its value. The advantage is not uniform; the notes record an unverified lead to one earlier paper that reportedly found no stronger effect for free items.

Percent-versus-amount framing matters less than popular advice suggests. The "Rule of 100" holds that percentages look bigger on low-priced items ([Jonah Berger](https://jonahberger.com/fuzzy-math-what-makes-something-seem-like-a-good-deal/)), but a summary of the underlying paper notes the low-price difference was directional only and not statistically significant ([Science Says](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/amount-vs-percentage-discount)). Use percent for cafe and bar tickets as a default, not a rule.

Across the surveyed products, the documented card contents converge on a minimum set: who the merchant is, one value headline, a validity cue, a single action, and terms one tap away. Amex's 2025 web layout, for instance, uses a "+" control, a "View Details" pop-up and a "View Offer Terms" link inside it ([Upgraded Points](https://upgradedpoints.com/news/amex-offers-layout-change/)). The FTC treats burying key limits in dense terms or behind tooltip buttons as a dark pattern ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers)), which argues for putting the three or four conditions that decide usability (what you get, valid until when, where, one per ticket, any minimum spend) on the card or first detail view in plain words.

### A real counter is defensible; a decorative one is a liability

Scarcity cues raise purchase intent on average, but the urgency variants are the weakest. One meta-analysis, read in a repository copy, found purchase likelihood higher under excess-demand framing than restricted-supply framing "but not urgency (limited quantity and limited time) scarcity" ([Ladeira et al. 2023](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/609e47cf-0367-4af7-82ac-9ab7154e2a2c/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Ladeira.pdf)), and a systematic review calls the quantity-versus-time comparison inconclusive ([Lancaster eprints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/233666/1/ScarcityMsgSLR_PURE.pdf)). These studies mostly measure purchase intent for paid goods; nothing found quantifies how a truthful remaining count changes claim rate for free perks. Expect a modest lift at best.

The regulatory line is clearer than the effectiveness evidence. The Princeton crawl of about 11,000 shopping sites found **1,818 dark-pattern instances on 1,254 sites**, including 393 countdown timers and 632 low-stock messages, with 234 instances outright deceptive, such as timers that reset on reload ([Princeton](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns)). The closest precedent to a perks wall is the CMA's action against the deals site Wowcher, whose timers and "Running out!" claims risked a misleading impression while most products stayed at a similar price afterwards; Wowcher undertook, without admitting a breach, to use only accurate timers, drop its permanent homepage timer, match scarcity claims to actual stock and sales, and refund about **£4.27 million to over 870,000 customers** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cma-secures-over-4-million-in-refunds-for-wowcher-customers)). That gives Vibe City a working test: every "N left" or "N claimed" figure must be reproducible from real claim data, the cap must be enforced, and the offer must end when it hits zero. Prefer a plain expiry line ("Valid until Sun 23:59") to an animated countdown. Aggressive dark patterns "generated a powerful backlash among consumers" in one experiment ([Journal of Legal Analysis](https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/13/1/43/6180579)), and the audience here is ticket holders the brand needs to keep.

## One tap to claim, a separate moment to redeem

Show the value before asking for anything. NN/g's guidance, drawn from observation across many usability tests without statistics, is that login walls are justified only rarely and that sites should give value first and ask at the moment the user decides to act ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/login-walls/)). In Baymard's survey, 19% of users said they abandoned a checkout because it required account creation; older Baymard-derived figures of 37% circulate with different years and wording ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment)). A ticket holder is already identified, so the wall should need no further sign-up, and any partner marketing consent belongs after the claim, optional and unticked; pre-ticked boxes were one of the practices Wowcher had to remove.

Manual code handling is the second well-documented friction point. NN/g observed users tapping promotional banners that did nothing, codes embedded in images that could not be copied, and incentives sent by email arriving too late, with the summary "the easier to apply the better" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/applying-discounts/)). Amex replaced an "Add to Card" button that reloaded the page on every click with an inline "+" for the same reason, though the "faster, cleaner" verdict is one writer's view, not user data ([Upgraded Points](https://upgradedpoints.com/news/amex-offers-layout-change/)).

Mature products separate two moments: claim (reversible, reserves the offer, changes the card) and redeem (consumed at the venue, final). Chase's wallet uses three status tabs, "Added", "Redeemed" and "Expired", and keeps redeemed and expired offers visible for 90 days ([Chase FAQ](https://www.chase.com/personal/merchant-offers/faqs)). The Entertainer has the merchant enter a 4-digit PIN at bill time, then displays a redemption code ([Entertainer support](https://support.theentertainerme.com/support/solutions/articles/157000368310-how-do-i-redeem-an-offer-)); per a snippet of its FAQ, the redemption can be cancelled until the PIN is entered. Bandwango, per help-centre snippets, lets an organiser choose between a venue PIN, GPS check-in, GPS-or-PIN as a fallback for poor signal, or a QR scan ([Bandwango help](https://help.bandwango.com/en/articles/11201556-redemption-methods-for-coupons-vouchers-activities)). For capped Vibe City offers this maps neatly: claim decrements the real count, redeem happens at the counter with a venue-validated step, and the validation yields the redemption data sponsors want. Showing a wristband has no friction, but it gives the organiser no data and cannot enforce a cap.

Expect a large gap between claims and redemptions. Mass digital coupon redemption in the United States sits in the mid-single digits; two aggregators citing the same industry source give 5.92% and about 7.0%, which conflict, so only the order of magnitude is safe to use ([Capital One Shopping](https://capitaloneshopping.com/research/coupon-statistics), [Voxbooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/digital-couponing-statistics-2026)). In-context mobile coupons do far better: one study found redemption probability for the same coupon ranging from about 30% to 80% depending on context ([Mills and Zamudio 2018](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11747-018-0592-7.pdf)), and a vendor case study reported 14% of in-store openers redeeming ([Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/ex/mobilecommercedaily/mondelez-captures-14pc-mobile-coupon-redemption-rate-for-in-store-shoppers)). Nearby partners and identified holders put Vibe City nearer the favourable end, but claims and redemptions should be reported as separate metrics. With capped inventory an unused claim also takes an offer away from another attendee, which supports a stated claim-expiry or release rule.

Location and timing drive redemption after the claim. The mall trial found that place and time of delivery significantly influence redemption and that mobile coupons are redeemed much faster than traditional ones ([Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/where-when-and-how-long-factors-that-influence-the-redemption-of-)). A randomised field experiment found mobile push lifted redemption rates by 6.0% versus pull ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/isre.2021.0206)); that is a measurable but modest gain. The evidence for wallet passes and lock-screen reminders is a 2017 vendor survey of stated intent, not behaviour ([Airship](https://www.airship.com/newsroom/urban-airship-analysis-of-retailers-mobile-wallet-coupons-and-loyalty-cards/)). The cheapest "wallet" is a "My offers" view next to the ticket, with the redemption screen showing the code or QR, the venue's address and hours, and the expiry together.

## Twelve offers need a uniform grid, not a carousel or filters

An offer wall is a homogeneous set: every card carries the same attributes. NN/g's guidance is that "card layouts are less scannable than lists" and poor for comparison because elements do not sit in predictable positions, while cards suit browsing mixed content ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cards-component/)). The fix is equal-height cards in a strict grid with each attribute at a fixed position, collapsing to a single column of compact rows on a phone. No empirical study of masonry (uneven-height) layouts was found; the case against it for a small comparable set rests on NN/g's reasoning that varying sizes weaken ranking and predictability, so treat "avoid masonry" as informed judgement, not measured fact.

Carousels hide partners. On Notre Dame's homepage carousel, about 1% of visitors clicked a feature and 84% of those clicks went to the first slide, by the primary author's account; a later article restates slightly different figures ([Erik Runyon](https://erikrunyon.com/2013/01/carousel-interaction-stats/)). User-driven product galleries are used far more heavily (about 72% of visitors interacted in one dataset from an author employed by a mobile-commerce vendor), yet roughly a third of users still dropped off at each step ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/02/carousel-usage-exploration-on-mobile-e-commerce-websites/)). Both datasets are from 2013 and 2015 and from contexts other than offer rails. NN/g advises five or fewer frames, no auto-advance, and "if you have a high number of items, use a list view instead" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-carousels/)). For a sponsor wall the commercial point is direct: partners placed late in a rail get materially less exposure.

Choice overload does not justify hiding offers at this scale. The well-known jam study compared 6 options with 24, and a 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect of "virtually zero" with considerable variance; both are cited from search excerpts and not from the papers ([Scheibehenne et al.](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)). Show all 3 to 12 offers. A featured slot is a steering device that will draw a disproportionate share of attention, so label why it is featured and rotate it if partner fairness matters; no study was found measuring featured-card or badge effects in small sets.

Filters are for long lists. NN/g frames them as a way to reduce a long list to a manageable set ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/filter-categories-values/)), and the festival precedents use an unsorted flat list. Up to about six offers, omit filters; at nine to twelve spanning bars, cafes and wellness, a single row of category chips built only from non-empty categories is enough, and category subheadings can replace filtering entirely. These thresholds are the researchers' inference; no source states a number.

For details, keep everything needed to choose on the card. NN/g advises against opening a product-detail page inside a bottom sheet and against modals for content unrelated to the user's goal, and says bottom sheets suit short interactions and need Back support and a visible Close button ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/bottom-sheet/)). Short terms and "how to redeem" steps fit a sheet on mobile or inline expansion on desktop. Motion should stay within NN/g's 100 to 500 ms range, never auto-advance, and drop non-essential movement under the reduced-motion setting ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/), [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)).

Two visual points carry extra caveats. On logos, only brand-guideline documents were found, all from search excerpts: they converge on equal sizing, optical balance and full colour as the preferred variant ([NHM brand guide](https://synthesys.info/content/nhm-brand-style-guide/en/home/accreditation.html)). A uniform neutral tile with the partner's name in text beside it is the researchers' suggested default, not a tested finding, and the belief that large perk apps use white logo tiles was not verified against screenshots. On theme, NN/g's review of the literature concludes that light mode gives better reading performance most of the time for normal vision, especially for small text, and recommends offering dark mode as a user choice ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)); the underlying studies used reading tasks, not card scanning. One of them found small dark-mode text much harder to read in simulated night-time conditions, which is relevant to an evening event, so small terms text needs checking in both themes.

Accessibility rules apply directly. Expiry and remaining quantity should be text, not only a timer, colour or progress bar, in line with the W3C timing criterion ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/timing-adjustable.html)); tap targets need at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels ([W3C](https://w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)); and "Claimed" or "Sold out" changes should be announced to assistive technology. The target-size and status-message wording comes from summaries, so check the normative text before writing acceptance criteria.

### Patterns to avoid, at a glance

| Pattern to avoid | Why | Strength of the case |
|---|---|---|
| Countdown or "N left" not driven by real data; permanent "ends soon" timers | Treated as deceptive by US, UK and EU regulators; Wowcher refunds | Strong (primary regulator release and Princeton crawl; legal originals not read) |
| Pressure copy and stacked urgency cues | Backlash to aggressive patterns | Moderate (one experiment, secondary) |
| Key conditions only in a terms link or tooltip | FTC treats as buried terms; hidden limits cause suspicion | Strong for guidance; no claim-rate measurement |
| Sign-up or data capture before the claim; pre-ticked marketing consent | Login-wall annoyance; Wowcher undertaking | Moderate (qualitative NN/g; self-reported Baymard survey) |
| Codes in images, or delivered only by email | Cannot be copied; arrive too late | Moderate (qualitative NN/g) |
| Carousel or rail as the main wall; auto-advance | Steep position effects; later partners unseen | Moderate (old datasets from other contexts) |
| Masonry for a small comparable set | Attributes land in unpredictable positions | Weak (reasoning, no measured study) |
| Filters that can return an empty result | Filters exist to shrink long lists | Weak (inference from guidance) |
| Expired or exhausted offers that still look claimable; no claim history | Top complaint type in reviews | Moderate (anecdotal review snippets) |
| Offer styling that reads as an advert strip | Banner blindness | Moderate (qualitative NN/g) |

## Competitive brief

### Executive summary

No surveyed product is a direct competitor to a ticket-unlocked local perks wall. The large ticketing apps treat extras as revenue: DICE Extras, Eventbrite add-ons and Fever add-ons are paid upsells, and no official page describing free third-party perks for ticket holders was found for any of them (all from snippets). The working precedents are low-tech: festival wristband discount pages and web-based destination passes. Membership and card-linked walls supply the best claim-state vocabulary, coupon marketplaces supply the cautionary tales about trust, and rewarded offerwalls supply little that transfers. Most product detail below comes from search snippets of help centres and trade press, so wording is approximate; only Chase, Amex (via one article), The Entertainer's redemption page, the Manchester Punk Festival page, RetailMeNot's press release and one offerwall vendor blog were read directly. Klook, Ticketmaster perks and UNiDAYS interface detail returned nothing usable.

### Opportunities

The free, ticket-unlocked partner wall is space the big ticketing apps have left open, and the festival programs that fill it are plain web pages with no data, no caps and no terms. Vibe City can keep their warmth and add what they lack: a claim state, a venue-validated redemption and real numbers for sponsors. A visible, truthful remaining count was not documented on any surveyed wall, and Amex's hidden cap shows the alternative, where holders discover the limit by missing out. A third-party tool offers an "Offer End" sort for card offers ([CardPointers](https://help.cardpointers.com/article/57-filtering-and-sorting-offers)), which implies demand for expiry visibility that the bank's own app did not meet at the time. Finally, RetailMeNot now leads with a promise that codes are checked before display ([RetailMeNot](https://retailmenot.mediaroom.com/2026-08-05-RetailMeNot-Launches-Rewards-App-Featuring-AI-Smart-Search-and-Industry-First-Working-Code-Promise)), which suggests "every offer here works" is a positioning asset for a small, curated local wall where the owner can confirm each offer in person.

### Threats

The largest threat is at the counter: staff who do not know the offer, a partner whose arrangement has lapsed, or a claim wrongly shown as used. Because the ticket implies Vibe City's endorsement, a failed partner offer damages the event brand as well as the venue's. The second is unofficial competition: around Coachella, restaurants run their own wristband deals outside the festival (snippet), and Cebu venues could do the same with no wall at all. The third is regulatory and reputational exposure from scarcity claims. The cases found are from the US, UK and EU, with a regional signal from Singapore that was not read in detail; no Philippine guidance was searched for or found, so local rules are an open question, not a clean bill. The fourth is the claim-to-redeem gap: sponsors who see claims but few redemptions will question the value unless both numbers are reported honestly from the start.

### Recommended actions for Vibe City

| Priority | Action | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build each card to one fixed template: benefit headline, partner name and logo beside it, plain-text expiry, real remaining count, one "Claim" button, key conditions visible | Face-value and zero-price evidence; NN/g on clear savings; common card anatomy across products |
| 2 | Adopt a three-state lifecycle (Available, Claimed, Used or Expired) with a "My offers" view next to the ticket, and validate redemption at the venue by PIN or QR | Chase, Entertainer and Bandwango models; gives sponsors redemption data |
| 3 | Make every number true and auditable: counters driven by live claim data, caps enforced, ended offers visibly closed or removed, no decorative timers | Wowcher undertakings; Princeton crawl; FTC guidance |
| 4 | Brief every partner's staff before each event and confirm each offer is live; give each offer a two or three step "how to redeem" | Review complaints about refusals and unrecognised codes (anecdotal snippets) |
| 5 | Keep the festival voice: name the program after the ticket, write each venue up like a recommendation, and avoid "coupon" language | Manchester Punk Festival and Great Escape positioning (one read, one snippet) |

## Research synthesis

### Method and limits

Three desk-research threads were combined: a survey of how existing products present offers, a review of evidence on claiming and redemption behaviour, and a review of layout guidance for catalogues of 3 to 12 items. No interviews, surveys, usability sessions or analytics from Vibe City attendees or partners were available. Evidence strength below uses three grades. Strong means a source read directly that reports observation or a regulator's decision. Moderate means academic findings reached second-hand, or expert guidelines without reported measurement. Weak means snippets, vendor claims, anecdotal reviews or the researchers' own inference.

### Key themes and supporting evidence

| Theme | Supporting evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| A concrete benefit, ideally a freebie, drives claims; the brand anchors its value | Zero-price study and freebie study (secondary; exact figures conflict across summaries); face value top driver in mall trial (abstract only); NN/g on unclear savings (qualitative) | Moderate. Headline order itself is untested |
| Truthful scarcity is acceptable and modestly useful; false scarcity is a legal and trust risk | Two meta-analyses and a systematic review (secondary); Princeton crawl and Wowcher release (read directly); FTC and CMA letter via summaries | Strong on the risk; moderate-to-weak on the benefit, and unmeasured for free perks |
| Friction before value suppresses action | NN/g login-wall guidance (qualitative); Baymard 19% self-report; NN/g on codes; Amex "+" change (one writer's view) | Moderate. No controlled test of step count on offer walls |
| Trust is won or lost at the point of use | Review snippets for Entertainer, Perkbox, Groupon; RetailMeNot's promise; a coupon company's survey claiming 35% of respondents had a code fail as expired, methodology unverified ([SimplyCodes](https://simplycodes.com/blog/coupon-statistics-2026)) | Weak-to-moderate. Consistent across sources, but anecdotal |
| Claim and redeem are separate moments; proximity and timing drive redemption | Chase, Entertainer, Bandwango flows; mall trial; push experiment (6.0% lift); wallet reminders (vendor stated-intent survey) | Moderate for proximity and timing; weak for wallet passes and reminders |
| Small homogeneous sets need a uniform, all-visible layout | NN/g cards guidance; carousel datasets from 2013 and 2015; choice-overload meta-analysis (snippet) | Moderate. Masonry case is reasoning only |
| The closest precedents are warm, local and low-tech | Manchester Punk Festival page (read); Great Escape, Sled Island, destination passes (snippets) | Moderate for existence; weak on how well they perform, since no results were found |
| Accessibility and theme choices affect small text and state changes | W3C criteria (one read, two via summaries); NN/g dark-mode review of reading studies | Moderate. No offer-specific accessibility research found |

### Insight to opportunity

| Insight | Opportunity for Vibe City | Impact | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free and face value pull hardest | Ask each partner for one genuine freebie or a clear headline value; lead the card with it | High | Low |
| Holders are already identified | No extra sign-up; one-tap claim tied to the ticket | High | Low |
| Offers fail at the counter | Partner staff briefing, live-offer check before each event, "how to redeem" on every offer | High | Medium |
| Claim and redeem differ | Available, Claimed, Used and Expired states plus "My offers" beside the ticket | High | Medium |
| No surveyed wall shows a truthful count | Live "N of M left" in text, driven by claim data, ending at zero | Medium (unmeasured) | Medium |
| Sponsors need proof | Venue PIN or QR validation; report claims and redemptions separately | High | Medium |
| Unused claims cost other attendees | Stated claim-hold period with release back to the pool | Medium | Medium |
| Position effects are steep | Uniform grid, all offers visible, rotate or label the featured slot | Medium | Low |
| Proximity and timing drive use | Redemption screen with address, hours and expiry; one opt-in reminder on event day | Medium | Low to medium |
| Festival voice builds belonging | Program name tied to the ticket; friendly one-line venue write-ups | Medium | Low |
| Small text reads worse in dark mode | Follow the device theme, test terms text in both, neutral logo tiles | Low to medium | Low |

### Recommendations in priority order

| Order | Recommendation | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Guarantee every listed offer works: confirm with partners, brief staff, remove or visibly close ended offers | The most consistent complaint in the evidence; protects the brand |
| 2 | Standardise the card: benefit, brand, expiry, real count, conditions, one button | Cheapest change with the broadest support |
| 3 | One-tap claim with no new sign-up; consent to partner marketing optional, unticked and after the claim | Removes the best-documented friction |
| 4 | Three-state lifecycle, "My offers" by the ticket, venue-validated redemption | Enables caps, history and sponsor reporting |
| 5 | Truth rules for all numbers and dates; plain-text expiry over countdowns | Keeps scarcity on the right side of regulators and users |
| 6 | Uniform all-visible grid, single column on phones, category chips only near a dozen offers, no carousel for the main wall | Fair partner exposure and scannability |
| 7 | Accessibility and theme pass: text for states and limits, adequate tap targets, reduced motion, both themes checked | Lower urgency, low cost |
| 8 | One opt-in event-day reminder; wallet pass as an optional extra | Weakest evidence; modest expected lift |

### Open questions for real user research

Does a benefit-first headline out-claim a venue-first one for Vibe City's audience, given that the festival precedents lead with the venue? Does a visible truthful count raise claims, or does it make a nearly exhausted offer feel not worth the trip? What claim-to-redeem rate do Cebu attendees actually produce, and how does it differ between event-day and following-week validity? Which validation method do Cebu bar, cafe and wellness staff handle best on a busy night: a PIN, a QR scan or a shown screen, and how reliable is connectivity at each venue? Do attendees understand "claimed" versus "used", and do they expect to be able to release a claim (no surveyed product was documented allowing this, and Chase explicitly does not)? How do partners want to be ordered and featured, and what do they consider fair? Do attendees want reminders at all, and through which channel? Does the wall read better in light or dark on phones at an evening outdoor event? What do Philippine consumer-protection rules say about quantity and time-limited claims? And how do real partner logos of mixed quality look on a uniform tile, which needs a screenshot review that this research could not do?

## Conclusion

The research points away from persuasion techniques and towards operational honesty as the real lever. Layout and framing choices matter at the margin, and most of them are cheap; the decisive variable is whether the offer on the card is the offer the bartender honours. That shifts part of the design problem off the screen and into partner onboarding, which is where a small local brand that knows its venues personally has an advantage no large platform can copy.

The evidence base is also thinner than the confidence of most published advice suggests. Almost nothing here was measured on an offer wall, and nothing on ticket holders in Cebu. Vibe City's own claim and redemption logs, split by offer type, card position and validity window, will within a few events be better evidence than anything cited above, provided the wall records both moments from the first event.
